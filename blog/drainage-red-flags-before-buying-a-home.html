Drainage Red Flags to Check Before You Buy a Home

September 12, 20264 min read

A drainage problem is invisible on the day you tour a house. The sun is out, the yard looks fine, the basement smells like fresh paint. Three months and one heavy storm later, you learn what the previous owner already knew. Water problems are among the most expensive surprises a homebuyer inherits, and a standard home inspection rarely calls them out.

Why Drainage Rarely Shows Up in an Inspection

A general home inspector checks that the sump pump turns on and that the gutters are attached. They are not there during a rain event. They do not test soil percolation. They are not evaluating whether the lot can move water away from the structure. Drainage is a wet-weather, whole-site problem. Most inspections are a dry-day, inside-the-house exercise. That gap is where buyers get hurt.

The house almost always tells the truth if you know where to look. Here are seven red flags worth a careful look on your walk-through.

The Seven Red Flags

  • 1. Stains or efflorescence on foundation walls. In the basement or crawl space, look low on the walls for horizontal staining, a chalky white mineral crust or a distinct water line. These are the fingerprints of water that sat against the foundation. Fresh paint on only the lower part of a wall is a tell, not a comfort.
  • 2. Grading that slopes toward the house. Stand at each corner and look at the ground. Soil should fall away from the foundation, roughly six inches over the first ten feet. On flat lots and settled clay, the grade often runs the wrong way and funnels roof and yard water straight to the wall.
  • 3. Downspouts that end at the foundation. One inch of rain on 1,000 sq ft of roof is roughly 600 gallons. If downspouts dump at the wall, onto a splash block or into a buried pipe that surfaces two feet away, that volume lands exactly where you least want it. Crushed or disconnected underground extensions are common and easy to miss.
  • 4. An existing French drain, sump pump or drywell. Existing drainage is not reassurance. It is evidence of a known water problem. Ask how old the system is, whether it still works and whether it solved the cause or chased the symptom. Gravel and fabric in clay soil silt up over time. A sump pump running during a dry week is telling you something.
  • 5. Fresh mulch, sod or landscaping in odd places. A thick new bed of mulch or a fresh strip of sod along one side of the house may be just that. It may also be covering a low spot that ponds after every rain. Be curious about new landscaping that hugs the foundation on one side only.
  • 6. Sticking doors, stair-step cracks and uneven floors. Chronic soil moisture makes clay swell and shrink. That movement shows up as diagonal cracks in brick or drywall, doors and windows that stick and floors that feel off. Not every crack is drainage, but moisture-driven foundation movement is one of the costliest outcomes of a water problem left alone.
  • 7. The lot next door, especially uphill. Water does not respect property lines. A neighbor whose lot sits higher, or whose downspouts and grading push runoff toward the home, becomes your problem the day you close. Walk the property line and picture where water would travel in a storm.

How to Check It in One Visit

  • Walk all four corners and note which way the ground slopes at each.
  • Trace every downspout to where it actually discharges.
  • Take a flashlight into the basement or crawl space and look low on the walls.
  • Ask the seller's disclosure directly: any prior water intrusion, and any drainage work done?
  • If it rained recently, look for the last places still wet or muddy.
  • Note any higher neighboring lot that drains toward the property.

If you can, go back after a heavy rain. Ten minutes in the yard during a storm tells you more than an hour on a sunny afternoon.

What a Red Flag Means for the Deal

One red flag is a question, not a deal-breaker. Several together mean you should price the fix into your offer before you sign, not after you move in. Get a quote from a drainage contractor during your inspection window.

Know what a real fix looks like. A bigger gutter or a load of topsoil is not one. On flat, clay-heavy lots, gravity systems keep failing because they need about 1% slope the site does not have, and their gravel and fabric clog. A pressure-fed system works differently: water moves plank-to-plank by hydrostatic pressure to a transition box or discharge point, so it runs at 0.0% slope with nothing to clog.
